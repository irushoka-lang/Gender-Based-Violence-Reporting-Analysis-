# Gender-Based Violence Reporting: Who Do Women Turn To, and Why?

## Project Overview

Gender-based violence (GBV) is widely underreported, meaning that official statistics may capture only a fraction of women's experiences. But **does it matter who collects the data and whom survivors turn to for support?**

This research examines how women disclose experiences of gender-based violence across state institutions, non-state organizations, and personal social networks. It explores how institutional affiliation and the context of disclosure influence the visibility of GBV, with implications for survey design, gender policy, and evidence-informed policymaking.

## Research Questions

- **Reporting pathways:** To whom do women report gender-based violence, and how do reporting patterns vary across countries?
- **Institutional differences:** How does reporting to state-affiliated institutions compare with reporting to non-state organizations and social networks?
- **Survey methodology:** Do NGO-led surveys capture different levels of GBV disclosure than government-administered surveys?

## Data and Scope

The analysis draws on two sources of evidence:

- **Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS):** Survey data from India, Ghana, Nigeria, and Kenya, combining available survey waves from 2005–2024 to examine women's experiences of GBV and their reporting behaviors.
- **India case study:** A comparison of GBV prevalence estimates from India's National Family Health Survey (NFHS-4, 2015–2016) and an NGO-led study conducted by the Society for Nutrition, Education and Health Action (SNEHA), in collaboration with University College London, in Mumbai.

The cross-country analysis examines self-initiated reporting, while the India case study explores differences in disclosure in surveys administered by state and non-state actors.

## Methodology

This project develops a framework that distinguishes GBV disclosure along two dimensions:

1. **Institutional affiliation:** State-affiliated institutions versus non-state institutions and social networks.
2. **Disclosure context:** Self-initiated reporting, when women voluntarily seek help, versus solicited reporting, when disclosure occurs in response to survey questions.

The analysis uses:

- **Descriptive statistics** to examine GBV experiences and reporting patterns across countries.
- **Regression analysis** to investigate individual-level characteristics associated with reporting to non-state institutions.
- **Comparative survey analysis** to explore differences in GBV prevalence estimates between government-led and NGO-led surveys in India.
- **Literature-based interpretation** to connect empirical patterns with research on underreporting, survivor-centered approaches, and gender-responsive data collection.

## Key Findings

- **Social networks matter:** Women frequently disclose GBV experiences to friends, family, and other people in their social circles before turning to institutions.
- **Non-state actors play an important role:** Reporting to non-state institutions was comparable to reporting to state institutions, with somewhat higher rates of non-state reporting in Kenya and Nigeria.
- **Reporting patterns reflect social inequalities:** Regression results indicate that women reporting to non-state institutions were more likely to have lower educational attainment, live in rural areas, and have more young children.
- **Survey methods may shape disclosure:** In the India case study, the NGO-led SNEHA survey recorded higher GBV prevalence than NFHS-4. However, differences in sampling, methodology, and target populations prevent attributing this difference solely to the identity of the data collector.

## Policy Implications

The findings highlight the importance of looking beyond official institutional records when measuring gender-based violence.

- Strengthen collaboration between governments, women's rights organizations, NGOs, and community-based organizations.
- Consider how survey design, interviewer training, and survivor-centered support may affect disclosure.
- Interpret prevalence estimates in light of the institutional and social contexts in which data are collected.
- Improve gender-responsive evidence systems so that marginalized women's experiences are better represented in policy design and resource allocation.

## Limitations

This study is exploratory and does not establish that non-state actors inherently produce more accurate GBV data or that women universally feel safer reporting to them.

The cross-country analysis identifies associations rather than causal relationships. The India comparison also involves surveys with different methodologies and sampling frames. Stronger causal evidence would require comparable surveys administered to the same population under controlled conditions.
